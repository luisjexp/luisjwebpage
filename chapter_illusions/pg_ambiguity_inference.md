# Ambiguity, Interpretation, and Inference

Many visual illusions happen because the image reaching the eye does not contain enough information to specify a single cause in the world. The brain has to interpret the image. Sometimes more than one interpretation is possible, and perception can switch between them.

The examples below are ambiguity-based illusions. As you look at each one, ask: what is physically present in the image, and what does my brain infer is present in the world?

# Illusions
---

## Necker Cube

```{figure} ../assets/necker_cube.png
:width: 460px
:align: center

Necker cube.
```

[Necker cube interactive demonstration](http://www.michaelbach.de/ot/sze-Necker/index.html)

If you look at the blue shape above, you will likely see a cube. If you stare at it for a while, you may notice sudden reversals between two different cube orientations. These reversals often happen every few seconds. In the [rotating Necker cube](http://www.michaelbach.de/ot/sze-Necker/index.html), the perceived direction of rotation can also reverse.

* Once you notice the reversals, see whether you can “hold” one interpretation. Usually, this is difficult.
* Try changing your gaze direction. This can sometimes bias the interpretation.
* Try blinking. Reversals can sometimes be triggered by eye blinks.


The line drawing itself does not change. What changes is the brain’s interpretation of a 2D image as a 3D object. Both cube orientations are plausible, which is why perception can flip between them.

## Schroeder's Stairs

```{figure} ../assets/staircase.png
:width: 460px
:align: center

Schroeder's stairs.
```

Notice how the image can appear either as stairs going from top left to bottom right, or as an upside-down version of that staircase. To experience the two interpretations, focus one at a time on the panels labeled “A” and “B” and try to see each as being in the foreground.

You should experience a switch between the two interpretations. In one, panel A appears closer and panel B farther away. In the other, panel B appears closer and panel A farther away.

Again, the image itself does not change. What changes is the brain’s interpretation of depth.

## The Missing Corner

```{figure} ../assets/missing_corner.png
:width: 460px
:align: center

Missing corner illusion.
```

[Missing corner interactive demonstration](http://www.michaelbach.de/ot/sze-missCornerCube/index.html)

**Effect**

The animation is usually seen at first as a bluish cube with one missing corner. But if you keep looking, you may discover other interpretations. For example, you may see a smaller cube attached to the corner, or you may see the inside corner of a room with a cube placed in it.

It can take time to see the alternatives.

**Comment**

Without strong depth cues, all of these interpretations are geometrically possible.

## Bistable Motion

```{figure} ../assets/motion_bistable.png
:width: 500px
:align: center

Bistable motion.
```

[Related discussion of perceptual ambiguity](http://www.slate.com/articles/health_and_science/science/2015/02/what_color_is_this_dress_a_scientist_explains_visual_ambiguity_and_color.html)


Which way are the dots moving: up and down, left and right, or in a circle?

Motion is also inferred. The visual system receives a sequence of changing positions over time, then groups those changes into moving objects. When more than one grouping is possible, the perceived direction of motion can become unstable.

## Dragon Illusion

[See the dragon illusion here](https://youtu.be/A4QcyW-qTUg).

Here the dragon may appear to turn its head and follow you as you move, even though the object is stationary.

**Comment**

This illusion depends on an ambiguous three-dimensional interpretation. The brain tends to assume the face is a normal outward-facing surface, but the actual object is folded inward. That wrong depth interpretation can make the head appear to rotate in an impossible way.

# Why Ambiguity Happens
**Many Objects, One Retinal Image**

Your only access to the world is through your senses. The visual system must take incoming information and build a useful representation of what is out there. Most of the time it does this remarkably well. But sometimes the available information is ambiguous: it can be interpreted in more than one way.

Our eyes receive a flat, two-dimensional projection of the three-dimensional world. Yet we usually experience a stable 3D world with a great deal of confidence.

This is a case of stimulus ambiguity: a single retinal image can be consistent with multiple, mutually exclusive interpretations.

Because the brain’s job is to build a stable and meaningful representation of the world, it must “choose” an interpretation. This process is often described as unconscious inference. How the brain does this is a central question in neuroscience.

Below is one example. These objects are very different from one another, yet from a certain viewpoint they can all project the same 2D image. This is the kind of problem the visual system constantly faces: the retina receives only the projection, while the brain must infer the object that caused it.

In all of these cases, the same retinal image could have come from different causes in the world. That means the same activity pattern in the retina can correspond to multiple possible objects or scenes.

---
Despite this ambiguity, humans are often very good at inferring the most likely cause. Ambiguity-based illusions are useful because they expose this inferential process. They create cases where two or more interpretations remain plausible, allowing us to see that perception is not just a passive copy of the image. It is an active interpretation of what probably caused the image.
